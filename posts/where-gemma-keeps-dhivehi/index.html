Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 1

October 5, 2026 · 9 min read

Summary. I tried to teach Gemma 4 Dhivehi by grafting a Dhivehi vocabulary onto it and fine-tuning. The model passed my agent tests and then answered a question about black holes with a news article about COVID. Before trying again on a larger model, I probed how Gemma 4 26B-A4B connects languages internally. Its middle layers hold a shared space where the same sentence in English, Hindi, Arabic, Russian and Japanese lands in nearly the same place. Dhivehi written in Thaana reaches that space, loosely. Dhivehi written in Latin letters, the way most Maldivians type it, never does. Across nineteen languages, whether romanised text reaches the shared space tracked the script more than how well-resourced the language is. I think this changes the order in which a new language should be taught: wire it into the shared space first, then teach it to talk.

Two scripts, one language

Dhivehi is the language of the Maldives. Its formal script, Thaana, is written right to left; each consonant carries an explicit vowel sign. Most people also write Dhivehi in Latin letters, mixed with English, especially in chat:

kihineh? miadhu kon kameh kurevidhaanee?

how are you? what are you up to today?

A useful Dhivehi assistant needs to read both, and answer in whichever the user wants.

The first attempt

General-purpose models spend about five tokens on an average Dhivehi word. Nau-1.0, an open Dhivehi model built on Gemma 4 E2B, added 32,000 Thaana tokens to Gemma's vocabulary and brings that to about 1.1 for common words:

Gemma 4 vocabulary Nau vocabulary
ކަމަށެވެ ("reportedly") ކ · ަމ · ަ · ށ · ެވެ ކަމަށެވެ
Tokens per word, top 10k words 4.89 1.07

Because Nau uses Gemma's original token ids plus the new ones, its rows can be grafted onto a bigger Gemma. I grafted them onto Gemma 4 E4B, trained the new rows together with a small adapter on Dhivehi news, then fine-tuned for chat and agent tasks.

On my agent tests the grafted model beat the version without the graft. In conversation it did this:

Me: hey can u explain what a black hole is, in dhivehi pls

Model: ކޮވިޑް-19 އަށް ފައްސިވާ މީހުންގެ އަދަދު ވަރަށް ބޮޑަށް އިތުރުވެފައިވާ ކަމަށް ހެލްތް ޕްރޮޓެކްޝަން އެޖެންސީ…

(The number of people testing positive for COVID-19 has increased sharply, the Health Protection Agency said…)

It invented words: about 12% of the Thaana words it wrote in a chat replay appear nowhere in an 82-million-word Dhivehi corpus, against about 1.3% for held-out human text. Its Latin-script glosses did not match the Thaana beside them. Two more rounds of supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning halved its cross-turn repetition and moved its translation scores a little. Its Latin reading and its multiple-choice score did not move.

My best explanation is the order of training. The new rows and the adapter were trained at the same time, on news only. While the rows meant almost nothing, the body adapted around them, in one register. Later fine-tuning could change behaviour on top of that. It could not change how the body represented Thaana.

Before trying again on Gemma 4 26B-A4B, a mixture-of-experts model with about four billion active parameters, I wanted to know how it represents languages it already knows, and where Dhivehi falls.

A shared space in the middle

I took twelve everyday sentences ("The weather is very hot today", "Where is the hospital?", "My phone battery is almost dead") plus "Which month is this?", in English, Hindi, Arabic, Russian and Japanese. For each layer I averaged the model's hidden states over each sentence's tokens. For each sentence, the centroid is the mean of the five languages' vectors (leaving out the language being scored). Then I asked whether each version is closer to its own sentence's centroid than to the other twelve.

Table 1. Centroid retrieval accuracy, 13-way (chance 0.08). Plain Gemma 4 26B-A4B, before any Dhivehi training.

From layer 4 to about layer 25, the five languages agree completely. Hindi written in Latin letters, the way people text it, agrees too. The first layers sort by spelling and script; the last layers turn back toward a specific output.

Thaana Dhivehi reaches the shared space at layers 7–10, though not tightly: its cosine to the centroid for "Which month is this?" was 0.74 at layer 10, against 0.92 for English and Hindi. Latin-script Dhivehi stays near chance at every layer.

A fish, a month

Dhivehi މަސް (mas) means both "fish" and "month". A logit lens passes each layer's hidden state at a word through the model's output layer and reads off the tokens it would predict. In "My mother cooked fish curry for lunch", translated into Dhivehi, the state at މަސް decodes at layer 25 to:

fish · Fish · fishing · मछली · 鱼

The Hindi and Chinese words for fish appear at the same layer as the English one. In "He stayed in Malé for three months", the same word decodes to month at layer 22. Given enough context, the plain model already picks the right sense.

It fails a harder pair:

މިއީ ކޮން މަހެއް؟

mee kon maheh? — which month is this?

މިއީ ކޮން ކަހަލަ މަހެއް؟

mee kon kahala maheh? — what kind of fish is this?

A Dhivehi speaker reads the second as fish. Nothing in the grammar forces it; people rarely ask what kind of month something is. At that word, the model ranked month 10th and 6th in the two sentences, and fish about 13,700th and 4,400th. It holds the relevant prior: in English, "what kind of fish" is ordinary and "what kind of month" is odd. It cannot apply that prior because މަހެއް is not connected to fish in its shared space.

Other words varied. ވާރޭ (rain) decoded to weather, rainfall, 雨. ހަކުރު (sugar) never came closer than rank 1,240.

Is it the script or the language?

Hinglish reaches the shared space, while Latin-script Dhivehi does not. I wanted to know whether that is because Gemma knows Hindi better, or because of something about the scripts.

I took 40 sentences from FLORES-200, which has professional translations of the same sentences in 200 languages, and romanised every language with the same mechanical romaniser (uroman). That holds the romanisation style constant. For each language I measured native↔Latin retrieval at the best middle layer (40-way, chance 0.025):

Script Better-resourced Less-resourced
Cyrillic Russian 1.00 Kyrgyz 0.81 · Tajik 0.76
Devanagari Hindi 0.78 Nepali 0.75 · Marathi 0.62
Greek / Georgian Greek 0.69 Georgian 0.65
Arabic Arabic 0.33 Pashto 0.17 · Sindhi 0.25
Ge'ez — Amharic 0.15 · Tigrinya 0.14
Thai / Lao / Khmer / Burmese Thai 0.14 Lao 0.15 · Khmer 0.14 · Burmese 0.07

Table 2. Native-script ↔ romanised retrieval. Native ↔ English retrieval was 0.75–1.00 for every language.

The model understood nearly every native script: native↔English retrieval was near 1.0 almost everywhere. It did not predict the Latin result (Spearman 0.33, p = 0.16). Within a script, the better-known language usually did a little better. Across scripts, the differences were much larger. Thai, a major language, scored below Kyrgyz.

The languages that connect write their sounds in ways that map closely onto Latin letters. Arabic usually leaves out short vowels; Thai, Lao, Khmer and Burmese have complex orthographies and no spaces between words. Thaana writes every vowel explicitly, much like Devanagari. Structurally, Latin-script Dhivehi should be learnable. The model has seen too little of it.

Romanisation style mattered too. In an earlier probe with hand-written everyday romanisations, Hinglish matched Hindi 100% and Arabizi matched Arabic 83% at layer 7, against 78% and 33% with uroman. The model learned the romanisations people type, not a formal scheme.

What I'm changing

This suggests a different order of training.

  1. New rows first, while the body stays frozen. Each new Thaana token is fit so that, in real sentences, the model's internal states with the new token match its states when reading the same word spelled in old pieces. The row starts where the model already places the word.
  2. Distil from Nau. Because the graft and Nau share a tokenizer, the graft can learn Nau's full next-token distribution at every position, not only the correct next token. The text is weighted toward Latin-script comments, chat and fiction, with only enough news to cover the vocabulary.
  3. Then, gently, the body. A small adapter, anchored to the original model's own predictions on English, plus a loss that pulls Dhivehi toward the shared space at middle layers only: Thaana and Latin versions of a sentence toward the cluster its English paraphrases already form, and uses of a homograph toward other uses of the same sense. The later layers, where register and script appear to live, are left free, so a casual Latin question can share a meaning with its English equivalent and still be answered in casual Latin.
  4. Grammar and conversation last, with the fine-tuning and reinforcement learning that already improved behaviour on the first model.

A check runs during training. It scores answers to held-out chat prompts for relevance and stops the run if off-topic answers climb, which is how the first attempt produced news articles.

Next

The training described above is running now. Part 2 will run the same probes on the trained model: whether Latin-script Dhivehi reaches the shared space, whether mee kon kahala maheh? comes out as a fish, whether ހަކުރު decodes to sugar, and how the result compares with the untrained 26B, the first grafted model and Nau on translation, Latin-script reading and agent tasks.

Experiment notes

Models. Gemma 4 26B-A4B-it (MLX 4-bit QAT build) for all probes, before any Dhivehi training. Nau-1.0 (Gemma 4 E2B with 32,000 added Thaana tokens), 8-bit MLX conversion, checked against the original weights on four translation tasks (chrF++ within 1.5 points).

Hidden states. Each sentence is run alone with a beginning-of-sequence token. Per layer, states are mean-pooled over content tokens. Retrieval is cosine nearest neighbour in both directions, averaged. Centroids are the mean of unit-normalised vectors of the other four of the five languages.

Sentences. Twelve everyday sentences plus "Which month is this?", hand-translated into Hindi, Arabic, Russian and Japanese, each with an everyday romanisation. Dhivehi Thaana from Nau's translation of the English (greedy decoding, repetition penalty 1.1); Dhivehi Latin built word by word from the most common human spellings in about 390,000 aligned transliteration pairs. FLORES-200 dev: 40 sentences, evenly spaced, 19 languages, uroman romanisation.

Logit lens. The hidden state at the last token of the target word goes through the model's final norm and tied output embedding. I report the rank of the English target token.

Limits. These probes use 12–40 sentences per language. Mean pooling mixes sentence length and content. The logit lens reads only what the output layer can decode; early layers can hold information it cannot show. One sentence set was translated by Nau, which makes its own mistakes: it rendered "sugar in my tea" as sugar in coffee.

Data. Dhivehi corpus statistics use d3b4g/dhivehi-corpus (CC BY 4.0). Latin-script comments from d3b4g/maldivian-latin-script-corpus (CC BY 4.0). Training also uses nau-ai/nau-sft and other research-use datasets; this is a research project. Evaluation sets are excluded from all training data.
