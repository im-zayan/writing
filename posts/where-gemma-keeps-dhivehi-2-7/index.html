Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.7: an idea to try next

October 9, 2026 · 18 min read

We can change how Gemma reads a word and which script it writes. These changes have not yet given us reliable Dhivehi answers.

I want to find which parts of the model caused those changes, then see if we can keep the gains without the damage. Experts might have separate jobs for understanding and grammar. We do not know yet.

Part 2.6 showed better next-token prediction after more Dhivehi training. The paragraphs were still poor. Here I return to the earlier tests of the model’s internal states. Most were small, and several used just one way of generating text. They give us leads to test. We have not yet tested the roles of individual experts.

A state swap can change a reading

A representation is a list of numbers the model uses to encode a token or sentence. In Part 1, we compared these vectors across languages. On a 13-meaning test, Thaana found its matching meaning more often in the middle layers than Latin Dhivehi did.

Thaana reached 13/13 at layers 7 and 10; Latin Dhivehi reached 3/13. The states matched on this set. That alone does not show that the model can answer a question about the sentence.

Part 1.5 tested use more directly. Gemma read a Dhivehi fish/month question as “What kind of month is this?” We replaced a state at the ambiguous word with a state from an English sentence. A fish donor could make the next word “fish”; a book donor could make it “book”. The explorer shows those saved measurements.

For the English fish donor, writing only the target word’s last subword piece favored fish over month through layer 12. Writing every piece extended that effect to layer 18. The swap changed the prediction in this test. It does not tell us where all understanding happens or which expert caused the change.

Alignment helped small probes

In Part 1.6, I asked to move the representations toward Sinhala. We kept fighting the model, and I wanted to use the route it already seemed to have.

We used the pretrained model’s Thaana states as the training target. Earlier tests placed those states near Sinhala. A frozen pretrained teacher read Thaana; the chat model read matching Latin Dhivehi. A small trainable adapter in the early layers pulled matching sentence and word states together. It trained on no Dhivehi answers. The architecture and losses are below.

For the swap row, we wrote Latin donor states into the unchanged model as it read Thaana. Four cases improved and one got worse. Without any swap, the model reading Thaana scored 14/19. The adapted states helped more than the stock Latin states, but the model still had the rest of the Thaana sentence to read. This did not test Latin understanding alone. The adapted model still got all five original questions about ambiguous words wrong when reading them directly in Latin.

In other checks, replies often addressed the input better, in Sinhala or romanized Sinhala. That fit the direction I had asked to explore. But it did not prove that the states moved closer to Sinhala. In one check, their cosine similarity to the Sinhala average fell even as meaning matches improved. What we intended and what we measured were not the same.

The first run damaged English and code cases. Run 2 added losses to limit that damage. We chose a checkpoint that passed those checks, using scores from 33 chat and 150 fiction items. Those same scores cannot also serve as a fresh test of the chosen model. We have not trained a control on mismatched sentence pairs, so we do not know how much the correct pairing mattered.

Other languages improved on the matching test

After the first alignment run, the model matched Arabic script with romanized Arabic more often: 32.5% → 65%. The score averages matching in both directions. Hindi rose 77.5% → 97.5%; Thai 13.8% → 33.8%. These are representation matches, not translation scores. The full comparison includes Greek’s small decline and Russian’s unchanged score.

For Arabic, the selected layer moved from 10 to 18, while native-script↔English retrieval stayed at 100%. Run 2’s selected checkpoint scored 51.2% on the script-matching test. The later run gave back some of the gain. Something helped beyond Dhivehi, but we do not yet know what.

An earlier run shows the limit of these scores. It brought the student’s states about 30% closer to the teacher’s. Yet its Dhivehi-to-English score stayed flat: 54.17 → 53.54 chrF++ on its own test of 96 sentences drawn from the training pool. Closer states did not produce better translations.

Changing the script could destroy the answer

We have already changed the reply language by changing internal states. We kept the request fixed, asked for answers in different languages, and swapped states between those runs. The reply language could switch. We did not establish whether it kept the meaning or grammar intact.

We then tried adding a direction computed from the model’s own Dhivehi versus Sinhala reply states. Here is the longer, 220-token evaluation on the same 150 Latin-Dhivehi prompts:

Intervention Thaana Meaning Repeats
None 37 25 12
Language direction ×1 64 19 50
Language direction ×2 113 2 109
Shuffled-label direction, same norm as ×2 86 3 90

All counts are out of 150. “Thaana” counts replies classified by script. “Meaning” counts a single model judge’s YES-or-PARTIAL label, with unparsed judgments counted as failures. “Repeats” is a mechanical flag for a low share of distinct three-token sequences, not a quality rating.

For the control, we shuffled the source language labels and built a direction of the same length. Changing the generation code also changed the baseline replies, so small differences between runs may be noise.

The stronger change produced more Thaana but far fewer answers that addressed the input. The shuffled control did much of the same damage. Counting Thaana replies gave the wrong impression.

This was a large change. The ×2 vector had length about 59, compared with 105 for the recorded average state length. We added it at nine final prompt positions and every generated token. This method failed. A smaller, more precise change might still preserve meaning while changing the reply language. We applied the vector at code layer 22, the 23rd decoder block when counting from one.

What I want to test next

Maarten Grootendorst’s visual guide suggested looking inside the mixture of experts. Our Gemma has 128 routed experts per layer and selects eight per token, plus an always-active shared feedforward network. A routed expert has about six million parameters. Earlier runs trained adapters on routed experts too. We have not tested what each expert does.

Multilingual Routing in Mixture-of-Experts reports language-specific routing near the beginning and end of the models it studied, with more cross-language alignment in the middle. That is a reason to study how Gemma chooses its experts. It is not evidence that our Gemma has a Dhivehi reader, a language selector and a grammar corrector as separate experts.

I would start from the successful state swaps and the failed steering experiment:

  1. Find what changed in the affected layers: attention, the shared feedforward network, or the routed experts. An expert firing often is only a clue. It may respond to the script, common tokens or the topic.
  2. Make small changes to those parts. Compare them with equally strong changes to unrelated parts. Check meaning, script and grammar separately on matched examples.
  3. Choose the parts and the strength of the change on one set of examples. Keep those choices fixed and test fresh examples. Use the old probes to find candidates, not to judge the final result.

The test is whether more answers say the right thing in good Dhivehi. If a small change helps, we can try training that part of the model. Better state matches or more Thaana characters alone are not enough.

Architecture and training details

These diagrams describe the earlier alignment adapter, not the continued-pretraining run in Part 2.6. The four diagrams show how the code works. The charts above show measured results.

The saved run attached 123 low-rank projections, with 11,151,360 trainable parameters. The pretrained teacher and the chat model’s original weights stayed frozen. Both used 4-bit model weights. There was no new tokenizer, Sinhala teacher or separate shared-space network.

Each batch held 24 sentence pairs. We skipped a pair if either side exceeded 96 tokens, including the start-of-sequence token, BOS. These alignment passes used plain text, without a chat template. At each of the three layers, the loss rewards the matching teacher state and penalizes matches to other batch entries. The temperature was 0.05. Sentence and word contrastive losses had equal weight.

The adapted student also read Thaana. A cosine-distance loss pulled its sentence and word states toward the teacher’s. Both student passes used the same adapter weights.

The training loss had three parts in the first run:

L = LLatin contrastive+ 0.3 LThaana cosine+ 10 KL(pstock ∥ pstudent)

We averaged the two representation losses across layers 7, 10 and 15. For the KL term, the unchanged chat model supplies detached next-token probabilities on English chat replay. The adapted model sees the same examples. The loss penalizes changes to those probabilities. This uses a full forward pass, but it does not teach Dhivehi replies.

Run 2 added 50 × Lanchor. On English, Spanish and code examples containing Dhivehi-looking Latin words, it kept each token’s state close to stock at layers 7, 10, 15 and 18. The loss penalized squared changes in each state, divided by the stock states’ mean squared length. The marked look-alike word received three times the token weight. Each step sampled eight anchor examples and added one code replay row alongside the two English replay rows. The coefficient 50 comes from the saved run configuration.

The adapter’s full training scale was 2; the half-strength checkpoints used scale 1. That scales the adapter’s contribution, not its rank or parameter count. To generate replies, we use only the chat model and its adapter.

Implementation and run details

Both runs used AdamW with zero weight decay, peak learning rate 10−4, 20 warmup steps and a cosine schedule. The first run requested 600 steps but stopped at 200 after preservation checks failed; the first-run charts here use step 100 at half strength. Run 2 requested 200 steps, stopped at 100, and its later selection chose step 50 at half strength. Checkpoints are therefore not interchangeable. The architecture is reconstructed from the saved trainer and run configurations, not inferred from the diagrams. Human layer numbers here are one-based; code taps 6, 9, 14 and 17 correspond to layers 7, 10, 15 and 18.

Data, controls and interpretation limits

The retrieval map is Part 1’s stock 13-meaning test. Its Latin Arabic is hand-romanized. The transfer chart is a separate 40-sentence-per-language FLORES test using uroman, with each model’s best layer selected within layers 4–20. Those percentages are not one benchmark, and the layer selection is exploratory.

The run-2 swap wrote states at 13 decoder layers (code indices 3–15, or layers 4–16 counting from one). We also tested other layer choices. We did not choose this setting in advance. The 9/19 versus 6/19 paired result has four gains and one loss; an unadjusted two-sided sign test gives p=0.375. The direct Latin result has five gains and no losses on the 19-item set. These small checks give us reasons to test further. They do not show that we restored general understanding.

The early alignment run, its later preservation variant, the older teacher-only run, the prompt-language patches and the steering sweep used different objectives and evaluation setups. They tested different parts of the problem. Their scores do not rank the methods fairly. We have not found an expert’s role or a reliable way to fix grammar.
