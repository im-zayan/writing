Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.7: an idea to try next

October 9, 2026 · 17 min read

All of this has brought me back to an earlier experiment, with a more specific question.

The last run got better at predicting held-out Dhivehi. Its paragraphs still fell apart. More training helped the loss, but did not tell us which part of the model needed help.

Reading Maarten Grootendorst’s visual guide to Gemma 4 made me wonder whether we could look more closely at the experts.

Our Gemma has 128 routed experts in each of its 30 layers. It selects eight per token, alongside an always-active shared expert. Each routed expert is a small feedforward network with about six million parameters. Our current run adapts attention and the shared feedforward path; the routed-expert weights and router weights stay frozen.

Could we find the parts that turn Dhivehi into a representation the rest of the model can use? Then the parts that turn that representation into an answer and choose its language, spelling and grammar?

We have already tested representations

In Part 1, I compared sentence representations across languages, layer by layer. Thaana sentences found their matching meanings in the middle layers on the small retrieval test; Latin Dhivehi struggled. The logit lens also exposed different readings of words such as fish and month.

Thaana reaches 13/13 at layers 7 and 10, while Latin Dhivehi reaches 3/13. The middle-layer match is evidence that related meanings have similar representations on this small set. It is not a general comprehension score.

Part 1.5 went beyond looking. Swapping an English word’s internal state into a Dhivehi sentence changed the translation in the fish/month probes. Swapping pretrained states into the chat model also corrected some misreadings. Those tests gave us causal evidence at particular layers and token positions, not a complete map of Dhivehi understanding.

Overwriting only the last piece with the English fish state favors fish through layer 12. Overwriting every piece extends that to layer 18. These boundaries belong to this sentence and this intervention; they do not establish a universal cutoff for understanding.

An early intervention already helped

In Part 1.6, we changed the early layers and improved Latin-Dhivehi understanding without training the model on Dhivehi replies. I had explicitly asked to move the representations toward Sinhala: we kept fighting the model, and I wanted to use the route it already seemed to have. Here is how we implemented that direction.

The actual training target was the frozen pretrained model’s representation of a sentence in Thaana, not a Sinhala translation or a Sinhala centroid. Our earlier measurements placed Thaana near Sinhala. The chat model read the same sentence in Latin Dhivehi. A rank-16 LoRA adapter changed attention and the shared feedforward network in layers 1–18. The routed experts stayed frozen.

At layers 7, 10 and 15, a contrastive loss pulled the chat model’s Latin representations toward the teacher’s Thaana representations, for both whole sentences and linked words. It pushed unrelated examples apart. A weaker loss also aligned the chat model’s Thaana states with the teacher, while an English KL loss discouraged changes to its existing English predictions.

There was no next-token training on Dhivehi answers. After changing how it represented the input, we let its existing generation machinery answer.

Inside the alignment adapter

The saved run attached 123 low-rank projections, with 11,151,360 trainable parameters. The pretrained teacher and the chat model’s original weights stayed frozen. Both used 4-bit model weights. There was no new tokenizer, Sinhala teacher or separate shared-space network.

The batch contained 24 paired sentences. If either side exceeded 96 tokens including BOS, the pair was skipped. These alignment passes used plain text, without a chat template. At each of the three layer taps, the correct teacher representation was the positive example and the other batch entries supplied negatives. The temperature was 0.05. Sentence and word contrastive losses had equal weight.

The same adapted student also read Thaana in another forward pass. Its sentence and word states were pulled toward the frozen teacher’s Thaana states using cosine distance. These two student passes share one set of adapter weights.

The training loss had three parts in the first run:

L = LLatin contrastive+ 0.3 LThaana cosine+ 10 KL(pstock ∥ pstudent)

The two representation terms are averaged across layers 7, 10 and 15. For the KL term, the unchanged chat model supplies detached next-token probabilities on English chat replay. The adapted model sees the same replay and is penalized for drifting from those probabilities. This uses a full forward pass, but it does not teach Dhivehi replies.

Run 2 added 50 × Lanchor. On English, Spanish and code examples containing Dhivehi-looking Latin words, it kept each token’s state close to stock at layers 7, 10, 15 and 18. The loss was a stock-energy-normalized squared distance; the marked look-alike word received three times the token weight. Each step sampled eight anchor examples and added one code replay row alongside the two English replay rows. The coefficient 50 comes from the saved run configuration.

The adapter’s full training scale was 2; the half-strength checkpoints used scale 1. That scales the adapter’s contribution, not its rank or parameter count. At inference the teacher and all training losses disappear. Only the chat model plus its adapter remain.

Implementation and run details

Both runs used AdamW with zero weight decay, peak learning rate 10−4, 20 warmup steps and a cosine schedule. The first run requested 600 steps but stopped at 200 after preservation checks failed; the first-run charts here use step 100 at half strength. Run 2 requested 200 steps, stopped at 100, and its later selection chose step 50 at half strength. Checkpoints are therefore not interchangeable. The architecture is reconstructed from the saved trainer and run configurations, not inferred from the diagrams. Human layer numbers here are one-based; code taps 6, 9, 14 and 17 correspond to layers 7, 10, 15 and 18.

The replies showed better understanding and often came out in Sinhala or romanized Sinhala. This was consistent with the direction I had asked us to explore. We wanted to make use of the model’s existing route to meaning, then deal with how it expressed the answer. We had not identified a specific Sinhala circuit, and Sinhala replies alone do not prove that the representations moved closer to Sinhala. One saved check actually showed raw cosine to the Sinhala centroid falling while matching-meaning retrieval improved. The intended direction, the training target and the measured geometry are different things.

The first run also damaged some English and code cases. A second run added English, Spanish and code examples containing look-alike words, held their representations close to stock, and added code to the prediction-preservation loss. Its selected checkpoint passed the tested English checks. That is the run used for the 9/19 swap result.

We also ran output-language patching and steering experiments. Changing the apparent language of an answer did not by itself establish better meaning or grammar.

The improvement crossed into other languages

The same alignment adapter also improved the match between romanized Arabic and Arabic script: 32.5% → 65%. Hindi went from 77.5% to 97.5%; Thai from 13.8% to 33.8%. Here are all 19 non-Dhivehi language rows in that saved comparison, including Greek’s small decline and Russian’s unchanged score.

For Arabic, the selected layer moved from 10 to 18. Its native-script↔English retrieval stayed at 100%. A later alignment checkpoint scored 51.2% on the Arabic script bridge. That was still above stock, but below the first run’s 65%. The gain was not monotonic.

This suggests that the adapter changed something useful for matching scripts across languages. We had not established the cause with a mismatched-pair training control. It is another reason to inspect the computation before calling the change Dhivehi-specific.

So the new idea is narrower: find out which computations inside those layers produced the effects we already saw. Whole-layer states mix attention, shared feedforward and routed-expert contributions. Can we separate their roles?

What I want to separate

Question Comparison to try
Does it understand the input? The same meaning in English, Thaana and Latin Dhivehi, with a fixed answer format
Does it use something shared across languages? The same facts and relationships expressed in different languages
What chooses the reply language? The same question with different requested answer languages
What helps the wording? Native-checked sentence pairs that preserve meaning but differ in grammar or spelling

There may be no clean boundary between these jobs. An expert may do several things, and one job may depend on many experts and attention heads. Gemma’s “shared expert” is simply active for every token; the name does not mean it contains a language-independent understanding of the sentence.

But there is a reason to look. Multilingual Routing in Mixture-of-Experts reports more language-specific routing in early and late layers, and more alignment across languages in the middle. Steering middle-layer routing toward task experts often used in English produced modest multilingual gains in the models they tested. That is evidence for a direction to investigate, not a result for our Gemma or for Dhivehi.

Watch first, then intervene

First, reuse the earlier representation and patching probes, then add fresh matched examples. Record which experts receive tokens, and with what weights. Compare stock pretrained Gemma, our CPT checkpoint and the chat-tuned model. Keep reading the input separate from generating the answer.

An expert lighting up for Thaana would only be a clue. It could be responding to the script, a common token or the topic. It would not yet be a “Dhivehi expert.”

Next, change a small part of the computation. Suppress a candidate expert’s contribution, or replace an activation with one from a matched successful example. Check whether the effect is specific: did understanding change, did the reply switch language, or did the wording improve? Compare against random-expert interventions, watch for general damage, and repeat on fresh examples.

We already have evidence that changing early representations can improve Latin-Dhivehi understanding. The next question is which computations produced that gain, and whether we can preserve it while changing the reply language and improving grammar. A clean separation between those roles remains a hypothesis to test.

If we can find a repeatable effect, we can try training the parts it points to. If we cannot, we will have learned something about the limits of treating whole experts as units of language ability.

That is where this has landed: a better question for the next experiment. We have already probed and intervened on whole-layer representations. What remains is the expert-level map and the targeted tests of routing and expert contributions.
