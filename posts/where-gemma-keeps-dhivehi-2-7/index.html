Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.7: an idea to try next

October 9, 2026 · 13 min read

All of this has brought me back to an earlier experiment, with a more specific question.

The last run got better at predicting held-out Dhivehi. Its paragraphs still fell apart. More training helped the loss, but did not tell us which part of the model needed help.

Reading Maarten Grootendorst’s visual guide to Gemma 4 made me wonder whether we could look more closely at the experts.

Our Gemma has 128 routed experts in each of its 30 layers. It selects eight per token, alongside an always-active shared expert. Each routed expert is a small feedforward network with about six million parameters. Our current run adapts attention and the shared feedforward path; the routed-expert weights and router weights stay frozen.

Could we find the parts that turn Dhivehi into a representation the rest of the model can use? Then the parts that turn that representation into an answer—and choose its language, spelling and grammar?

We have already tested representations

In Part 1, I compared sentence representations across languages, layer by layer. Thaana sentences found their matching meanings in the middle layers on the small retrieval test; Latin Dhivehi struggled. The logit lens also exposed different readings of words such as fish and month.

Thaana reaches 13/13 at layers 7 and 10, while Latin Dhivehi reaches 3/13. The middle-layer match is evidence that related meanings have similar representations on this small set. It is not a general comprehension score.

Part 1.5 went beyond looking. Swapping an English word’s internal state into a Dhivehi sentence changed the translation in the fish/month probes. Swapping pretrained states into the chat model also corrected some misreadings. Those tests gave us causal evidence at particular layers and token positions, not a complete map of Dhivehi understanding.

Overwriting only the last piece with the English fish state favors fish through layer 12. Overwriting every piece extends that to layer 18. These boundaries belong to this sentence and this intervention; they do not establish a universal cutoff for understanding.

Then Part 1.6 trained an adapter to align Latin Dhivehi with the pretrained model’s Thaana representations. Retrieval improved, but replies often came out in Sinhala. We also ran output-language patching and steering experiments. Changing the apparent language of an answer did not by itself establish better meaning or grammar.

The improvement crossed into other languages

The same alignment adapter also improved the match between romanized Arabic and Arabic script: 32.5% → 65%. Hindi went from 77.5% to 97.5%; Thai from 13.8% to 33.8%. Here are all 19 non-Dhivehi language rows in that saved comparison, including Greek’s small decline and Russian’s unchanged score.

For Arabic, the selected layer moved from 10 to 18. Its native-script↔English retrieval stayed at 100%. A later alignment checkpoint scored 51.2% on the Arabic script bridge—still above stock, below the first run’s 65%. The gain was not monotonic.

This suggests that the adapter changed something useful for matching scripts across languages. We had not established the cause with a mismatched-pair training control. It is another reason to inspect the computation before calling the change Dhivehi-specific.

Chart data and limits

The charts use saved experiments, with no new model run. The retrieval map comes from the stock rerun of Part 1’s 13-meaning test; “Latin Arabic” there uses the hand-written romanizations from that test. The transfer chart is a separate FLORES test using uroman. Do not compare their percentages as one benchmark. The adapter in the transfer chart is the earlier representation-alignment adapter, not the CPT checkpoint from Part 2.6. Best-layer selection is exploratory, not a held-out layer choice. The patch explorer covers one target and three donor conditions; it does not locate expert identities or demonstrate grammatical correction.

So the new idea is narrower: find out which computations inside those layers produced the effects we already saw. Whole-layer states mix attention, shared feedforward and routed-expert contributions. Can we separate their roles?

What I want to separate

Question Comparison to try
Does it understand the input? The same meaning in English, Thaana and Latin Dhivehi, with a fixed answer format
Does it use something shared across languages? The same facts and relationships expressed in different languages
What chooses the reply language? The same question with different requested answer languages
What helps the wording? Native-checked sentence pairs that preserve meaning but differ in grammar or spelling

There may be no clean boundary between these jobs. An expert may do several things, and one job may depend on many experts and attention heads. Gemma’s “shared expert” is simply active for every token; the name does not mean it contains a language-independent understanding of the sentence.

But there is a reason to look. Multilingual Routing in Mixture-of-Experts reports more language-specific routing in early and late layers, and more alignment across languages in the middle. Steering middle-layer routing toward task experts often used in English produced modest multilingual gains in the models they tested. That is evidence for a direction to investigate, not a result for our Gemma or for Dhivehi.

Watch first, then intervene

First, reuse the earlier representation and patching probes, then add fresh matched examples. Record which experts receive tokens, and with what weights. Compare stock pretrained Gemma, our CPT checkpoint and the chat-tuned model. Keep reading the input separate from generating the answer.

An expert lighting up for Thaana would only be a clue. It could be responding to the script, a common token or the topic. It would not yet be a “Dhivehi expert.”

Next, change a small part of the computation. Suppress a candidate expert’s contribution, or replace an activation with one from a matched successful example. Check whether the effect is specific: did understanding change, did the reply switch language, or did the wording improve? Compare against random-expert interventions, watch for general damage, and repeat on fresh examples.

The result I would like to find is a separation: an early intervention restores understanding of Latin Dhivehi; a later one changes the reply language while preserving its meaning. Grammar may turn out to depend on a different, overlapping set of components. Those are hypotheses to test.

If we can find a repeatable effect, we can try training the parts it points to. If we cannot, we will have learned something about the limits of treating whole experts as units of language ability.

That is where this has landed: a better question for the next experiment. We have already probed and intervened on whole-layer representations. What remains is the expert-level map and the targeted tests of routing and expert contributions.
