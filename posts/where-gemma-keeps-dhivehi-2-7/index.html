Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.7: an idea to try next

October 9, 2026 · 13 min read

I’m trying to make Gemma, a language model, read ordinary Dhivehi messages and write useful replies. One early test shows the problem:

Intended meaningWhat kind of fish is this?

Gemma’s translationWhat kind of month is this?

A Dhivehi word can refer to a fish or a month. A speaker uses the sentence to tell which meaning fits. Gemma chose the wrong one.

Dhivehi also has its own script, Thaana. In messages, people often use Latin letters, as I’m doing here in English. Gemma struggles even more with that spelling. It may miss the meaning, answer in another language, or string Dhivehi words together without making sense.

We tried changing how it reads and how it writes. The results led to a more specific question: can we find the parts that help, and change those without breaking the rest?

Changing a reading

First, I wanted to know whether we could change the fish/month reading inside the model.

Gemma processes text through 30 layers of calculations. At each layer it stores numbers for each piece of text. Those numbers are called its state. Later layers use those numbers to decide what to write.

We took the state for “fish” from an English sentence and put it where Gemma was processing the ambiguous Dhivehi word. The input sentence stayed the same. After the fixed translation opening “What kind of”, the model’s next prediction switched from “month” to “fish”. A state taken from “book” could make it predict “book” instead.

That was a useful result. Changing those numbers changed the prediction. It did not tell us which small part of the model produced the numbers, or whether the same change would help other sentences.

Try the saved fish/month experiment

The controls below replay saved results. They do not run a model. Gemma splits a word into smaller pieces, called tokens; the second control chooses whether to replace the state at one piece or all of them.

The “fish” state favored fish over month through layer 12 when we changed only the final piece. Changing every piece extended that to layer 18. Those limits belong to this test, not to understanding in general.

Teaching it to read Latin Dhivehi

A one-off state swap is not much use in a chatbot. We needed the model to make a better reading on its own.

We had found that the version of Gemma from before chat training read Thaana better than the chat version. So we used it as a teacher. The teacher read a sentence in Thaana. The chat model read the same sentence in Latin letters. We trained a small add-on, called an adapter, to bring the chat model’s internal states closer to the teacher’s.

We did not train it on Dhivehi replies. We changed how it processed the input, then let it answer with the machinery it already had.

I had asked us to move toward Sinhala, another language the model could use, because we kept fighting its existing behavior. Our earlier tests placed its Thaana states near its Sinhala states. The training target was still the teacher’s Thaana states, not Sinhala sentences.

There were signs of progress. In one small test, the adapted model used the intended sense word in 5 of 19 Latin-Dhivehi translations, up from 0 of 19. Some replies addressed the message better, in Sinhala or Sinhala written in Latin letters. Using that route was deliberate. Getting the answer back into good Dhivehi was still unfinished work.

The gains were limited. The five original questions about ambiguous words were still wrong. The first run also damaged English and code cases. We added checks and training terms to limit that damage in a second run. This was enough to keep investigating, not enough to call the problem solved.

There was a further clue: after the first run, the model also got better at matching Arabic sentences with the same sentences written in Latin letters. Its score rose from 32.5% to 65%. That test checked whether sentence states matched, not whether it could translate them. The change appeared to help more than Dhivehi, but we had not isolated why.

Sinhala replies did not prove that states moved closer to Sinhala. In one check, their cosine similarity to the Sinhala average fell while meaning matches improved. What we intended and what we measured were different.

A separate earlier run also showed the limits of matching states. It reduced the measured teacher–student gap by about 30%, but its Dhivehi-to-English score stayed flat: 54.17 → 53.54 chrF++ on a 96-sentence training-progress test drawn from the training pool.

More Thaana, worse answers

Next we tried to change the output language more directly.

We compared the model’s internal states while it wrote Dhivehi and Sinhala. From the difference, we built a change to add as it generated each new piece of text. The hope was to keep its reading of the message and make it write Dhivehi.

The strongest setting looked successful if we counted scripts alone. Replies in Thaana rose from 37 to 113 out of 150. But replies judged to address the input, even partly, fell from 25 to 2. Repetition flags rose from 12 to 109.

We had changed the appearance of the answers and damaged their content.

A control made the lesson clearer. We mixed up which source replies we called Dhivehi and Sinhala, then built a change of the same size. That also produced more Thaana and mostly poor answers. Much of the effect could come from disrupting the model, rather than finding a clean control for Dhivehi.

This does not mean the job is impossible. The change was large, and we applied it at every step of the reply. It tells us that this method failed. We need to try something smaller and check the meaning as well as the script.

In a separate test, we kept the request fixed, asked for different reply languages and swapped states between the runs. The reply language could switch. That showed some causal control in that prompt setup, but did not establish that meaning and grammar survived.

The next idea: look inside the layers

Reading Maarten Grootendorst’s visual guide to Gemma 4 brought me back to how the model is built.

Our Gemma is a mixture of experts. The name sounds grander than the parts: each expert is a small neural network inside a layer. A router chooses eight of 128 experts to process each token. The model also uses a shared network for every token. A token is a piece of text, often smaller than a word.

These experts were not given jobs such as “read Dhivehi” or “fix grammar”. Their roles, if they have clear ones, arose during training. We have trained adapters on routed experts in other runs, but we have not tested what each one does.

So the question is now smaller: which parts carried the fish state through the model, and could changing those parts help it read other sentences?

I want to trace the English-fish-to-Dhivehi swap first. Does the effect pass through attention, which lets tokens use context from other tokens? Through the shared network? Or through a few routed experts? Then change those parts alone, compare them with changes to unrelated parts, and test on new sentences.

Research on multilingual expert routing gives us a reason to look: in the models studied, early and late layers used experts differently across languages, while middle-layer choices overlapped more. It does not tell us what Gemma’s experts do for Dhivehi.

If a small change helps the model read the message and answer it in good Dhivehi, we can try training that part. That is the next experiment. We have not run it yet.

Technical appendix: how we trained the alignment adapter

These diagrams describe the earlier alignment adapter, not the continued-pretraining run in Part 2.6. The four diagrams show how the code works. The charts above show measured results.

The saved run attached 123 low-rank projections, with 11,151,360 trainable parameters. The pretrained teacher and the chat model’s original weights stayed frozen. Both used 4-bit model weights. There was no new tokenizer, Sinhala teacher or separate shared-space network.

Each batch held 24 sentence pairs. We skipped a pair if either side exceeded 96 tokens, including the start-of-sequence token, BOS. These alignment passes used plain text, without a chat template. At each of the three layers, the loss rewards the matching teacher state and penalizes matches to other batch entries. The temperature was 0.05. Sentence and word contrastive losses had equal weight.

The adapted student also read Thaana. A cosine-distance loss pulled its sentence and word states toward the teacher’s. Both student passes used the same adapter weights.

The training loss had three parts in the first run:

L = LLatin contrastive+ 0.3 LThaana cosine+ 10 KL(pstock ∥ pstudent)

We averaged the two representation losses across layers 7, 10 and 15. For the KL term, the unchanged chat model supplies detached next-token probabilities on English chat replay. The adapted model sees the same examples. The loss penalizes changes to those probabilities. This uses a full forward pass, but it does not teach Dhivehi replies.

Run 2 added 50 × Lanchor. On English, Spanish and code examples containing Dhivehi-looking Latin words, it kept each token’s state close to stock at layers 7, 10, 15 and 18. The loss penalized squared changes in each state, divided by the stock states’ mean squared length. The marked look-alike word received three times the token weight. Each step sampled eight anchor examples and added one code replay row alongside the two English replay rows. The coefficient 50 comes from the saved run configuration.

The adapter’s full training scale was 2; the half-strength checkpoints used scale 1. That scales the adapter’s contribution, not its rank or parameter count. To generate replies, we use only the chat model and its adapter.

Implementation and run details

Both runs used AdamW with zero weight decay, peak learning rate 10−4, 20 warmup steps and a cosine schedule. The first run requested 600 steps but stopped at 200 after preservation checks failed; the first-run charts here use step 100 at half strength. Run 2 requested 200 steps, stopped at 100, and its later selection chose step 50 at half strength. Checkpoints are therefore not interchangeable. The architecture is reconstructed from the saved trainer and run configurations, not inferred from the diagrams. Human layer numbers here are one-based; code taps 6, 9, 14 and 17 correspond to layers 7, 10, 15 and 18.

Data and statistical notes

The retrieval map is Part 1’s stock 13-meaning test. Its Latin Arabic is hand-romanized. The transfer chart is a separate 40-sentence-per-language FLORES test using uroman, with each model’s best layer selected within layers 4–20. Those percentages are not one benchmark, and the layer selection is exploratory.

The run-2 swap wrote states at 13 decoder layers (code indices 3–15, or layers 4–16 counting from one). We also tested other layer choices. We did not choose this setting in advance. The 9/19 versus 6/19 paired result has four gains and one loss; an unadjusted two-sided sign test gives p=0.375. The direct Latin result has five gains and no losses on the 19-item set. These small checks give us reasons to test further. They do not show that we restored general understanding.

The early alignment run, its later preservation variant, the older teacher-only run, the prompt-language patches and the steering sweep used different objectives and evaluation setups. They tested different parts of the problem. Their scores do not rank the methods fairly. We have not found an expert’s role or a reliable way to fix grammar.
