Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.7: an idea to try next

October 9, 2026 · 18 min read

We can change a word’s reading inside Gemma. We can also change which script it writes. We have not yet turned those interventions into reliable Dhivehi answers.

That is the reason to look more closely at the experts. I want to find the smaller computations behind the changes we have already seen, then test whether we can keep the gains without the damage. Separate “understanding” and “grammar” experts are a possibility to investigate, not something we have found.

Part 2.6 reported lower loss after more Dhivehi pretraining, alongside poor paragraphs. This post returns to the earlier representation experiments. The results are exploratory: small probes, selected checkpoints and one decoding setup in several tests. The expert-level study described at the end has not run.

A state swap can change a reading

A representation is the vector of numbers the model carries for a token or sentence. In Part 1, we compared these vectors across languages. On a 13-meaning test, Thaana found its matching meaning more often in the middle layers than Latin Dhivehi did.

Thaana reached 13/13 at layers 7 and 10; Latin Dhivehi reached 3/13. This is evidence of matching representations on that set. It does not establish that the model can use the meaning to answer a question.

Part 1.5 tested use more directly. Gemma read a Dhivehi fish/month question as “What kind of month is this?” We replaced a state at the ambiguous word with a state from an English sentence. A fish donor could make the next word “fish”; a book donor could make it “book”. The explorer shows those saved measurements.

For the English fish donor, writing only the target word’s last subword piece favored fish over month through layer 12. Writing every piece extended that effect to layer 18. This demonstrates a causal effect in one probe. It does not establish a universal cutoff for understanding or locate a particular expert.

Alignment helped small probes

In Part 1.6, I explicitly asked to move the representations toward Sinhala. We kept fighting the model, and I wanted to use the route it already seemed to have.

The implemented target was the pretrained model’s representation of Thaana, which earlier probes placed near Sinhala. A frozen pretrained teacher read Thaana; the chat model read matching Latin Dhivehi. A small trainable adapter in the early layers pulled matching sentence and word states together. It trained on no Dhivehi answers. The architecture and losses are below.

Run 1 here is step 100 at half adapter strength; run 2 is its selected step-50 checkpoint at half strength. The 12-item row measures retrieval. The 19-item rows use a keyword-based sense check, not a full translation-quality judgment.

For the swap row, Latin donor states were written into the untouched model while it read Thaana. The 9-versus-6 result contains four improvements and one regression. Without any swap, the model reading Thaana scored 14/19. The adapted states carried more useful information than stock Latin states in this small test, but the recipient still had Thaana context. This is not standalone Latin understanding. The five original homograph questions remained wrong in the adapted model’s direct Latin translations.

Replies in other checks often showed a better grasp of the input, expressed in Sinhala or romanized Sinhala. That was consistent with the direction I had asked to explore. It did not prove that representations moved geometrically closer to Sinhala: one check showed raw cosine to the Sinhala centroid falling while matching-meaning retrieval rose. Intent, training target and measured geometry are different things.

The first run damaged English and code cases. Run 2 added preservation losses and selected a checkpoint that passed the tested thresholds. Its generation scores came from the same 33 chat and 150 fiction items used to select that checkpoint, so they are not an independent confirmation. We have not run a mismatched-pair training control to isolate what the alignment objective contributed.

The matching gain crossed into other languages

Arabic native-script↔romanized retrieval rose 32.5% → 65% after the first alignment run. Hindi rose 77.5% → 97.5%; Thai 13.8% → 33.8%. These are representation matches, not translation scores. The full comparison includes Greek’s small decline and Russian’s unchanged score.

For Arabic, the selected layer moved from 10 to 18, while native-script↔English retrieval stayed at 100%. Run 2’s selected checkpoint scored 51.2% on the script-matching test. The gain was not monotonic. This suggests a change useful beyond Dhivehi, but does not identify its cause.

There is also an earlier counterexample. A separate teacher-alignment run reduced its measured state gap by about 30%, while its own 96-sentence Dhivehi-to-English meter, carved from the training pool, stayed flat: 54.17 → 53.54 chrF++. Moving representations closer is an incomplete objective for useful language behavior.

Changing the script could destroy the answer

We already have some causal evidence about output-language control. In a separate experiment, we kept the request fixed and changed the language instruction. Patching states between those runs could change the reply language. That located a controllable part of the computation in that prompt setup; it did not establish that meaning and grammar survived.

We then tried adding a direction computed from the model’s own Dhivehi versus Sinhala reply states. Here is the longer, 220-token evaluation on the same 150 Latin-Dhivehi prompts:

Intervention Thaana Meaning Repeats
None 37 25 12
Language direction ×1 64 19 50
Language direction ×2 113 2 109
Shuffled-label direction, same norm as ×2 86 3 90

All counts are out of 150. “Thaana” counts replies classified by script. “Meaning” counts a single model judge’s YES-or-PARTIAL label, with unparsed judgments counted as failures. “Repeats” is a mechanical flag for a low share of distinct three-token sequences, not a quality rating. The shuffled control mixes the source language labels before building the direction, then matches its norm. A different generation implementation changed the unsteered baseline, so small cross-run differences should not be read as gains.

The stronger intervention produced more Thaana and far fewer answers that addressed the input. The shuffled control did much of the same damage. Script compliance alone was misleading.

But this was a large, sustained perturbation: the ×2 vector had norm about 59, against a recorded mean residual-state norm of 105. It was added at nine final prompt positions and every generated token. This steering recipe failed. It does not show that meaning and output language are inseparable. The vector was applied at code layer 22, the 23rd decoder block in one-based numbering.

What I want to test next

Maarten Grootendorst’s visual guide suggested looking inside the mixture of experts. Our Gemma has 128 routed experts per layer and selects eight per token, plus an always-active shared feedforward network. A routed expert has about six million parameters. Earlier lanes already trained routed-expert adapters; what we have not done is map their roles through causal tests.

Multilingual Routing in Mixture-of-Experts reports language-specific routing near the beginning and end of the models it studied, with more cross-language alignment in the middle. That makes expert routing worth investigating. It is not evidence that our Gemma has a Dhivehi reader, a language selector and a grammar corrector as separate experts.

I would start from the successful state swaps and the failed steering experiment:

  1. Trace the affected layers, separating attention, the shared feedforward path and routed-expert contributions. Routing frequency alone is only a clue; it can reflect script, token frequency or topic.
  2. Make smaller, measured interventions in the candidates. Compare matched examples and matched-strength sham interventions. Test preservation of meaning separately from script and grammar.
  3. Choose the components and intervention strength on a discovery set, then freeze those choices and test fresh examples. The old probes help find candidates; they should not also supply the final verdict.

The result to chase is more correct Dhivehi answers with the intended meaning preserved. If a targeted intervention achieves that, it gives us a training target. If only the representation score or script count moves, we have not solved the problem.

Architecture and training details

These diagrams describe the earlier alignment adapter, not the continued-pretraining run in Part 2.6. All four are schematics of the code; the retrieval map and transfer chart above are measured results.

The saved run attached 123 low-rank projections, with 11,151,360 trainable parameters. The pretrained teacher and the chat model’s original weights stayed frozen. Both used 4-bit model weights. There was no new tokenizer, Sinhala teacher or separate shared-space network.

The batch contained 24 paired sentences. If either side exceeded 96 tokens including BOS, the pair was skipped. These alignment passes used plain text, without a chat template. At each of the three layer taps, the correct teacher representation was the positive example and the other batch entries supplied negatives. The temperature was 0.05. Sentence and word contrastive losses had equal weight.

The same adapted student also read Thaana in another forward pass. Its sentence and word states were pulled toward the frozen teacher’s Thaana states using cosine distance. These two student passes share one set of adapter weights.

The training loss had three parts in the first run:

L = LLatin contrastive+ 0.3 LThaana cosine+ 10 KL(pstock ∥ pstudent)

The two representation terms are averaged across layers 7, 10 and 15. For the KL term, the unchanged chat model supplies detached next-token probabilities on English chat replay. The adapted model sees the same replay and is penalized for drifting from those probabilities. This uses a full forward pass, but it does not teach Dhivehi replies.

Run 2 added 50 × Lanchor. On English, Spanish and code examples containing Dhivehi-looking Latin words, it kept each token’s state close to stock at layers 7, 10, 15 and 18. The loss was a stock-energy-normalized squared distance; the marked look-alike word received three times the token weight. Each step sampled eight anchor examples and added one code replay row alongside the two English replay rows. The coefficient 50 comes from the saved run configuration.

The adapter’s full training scale was 2; the half-strength checkpoints used scale 1. That scales the adapter’s contribution, not its rank or parameter count. At inference the teacher and all training losses disappear. Only the chat model plus its adapter remain.

Implementation and run details

Both runs used AdamW with zero weight decay, peak learning rate 10−4, 20 warmup steps and a cosine schedule. The first run requested 600 steps but stopped at 200 after preservation checks failed; the first-run charts here use step 100 at half strength. Run 2 requested 200 steps, stopped at 100, and its later selection chose step 50 at half strength. Checkpoints are therefore not interchangeable. The architecture is reconstructed from the saved trainer and run configurations, not inferred from the diagrams. Human layer numbers here are one-based; code taps 6, 9, 14 and 17 correspond to layers 7, 10, 15 and 18.

Data, controls and interpretation limits

The retrieval map is Part 1’s stock 13-meaning test. Its Latin Arabic is hand-romanized. The transfer chart is a separate 40-sentence-per-language FLORES test using uroman, with each model’s best layer selected within layers 4–20. Those percentages are not one benchmark, and the layer selection is exploratory.

The run-2 swap repeatedly overwrote states at 13 decoder layers (code indices 3–15, one-based layers 4–16). It is one intervention among the layer sweep, not a preselected universal operating point. The 9/19 versus 6/19 paired result has four gains and one loss; an unadjusted two-sided sign test gives p=0.375. The direct Latin result has five gains and no losses on the 19-item set. These small checks motivate further tests, not a general recovery claim.

The early alignment run, its later preservation variant, the older teacher-only run, the prompt-language patches and the steering sweep used different objectives and evaluation setups. They are evidence about different parts of the problem, not one controlled ranking of methods. No individual expert role or reliable grammar intervention has been established.
