Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2: write it in Thaana first

October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

Summary. Part 1 found that Gemma 4 understands Dhivehi written in Thaana but not Dhivehi typed in Latin letters, which is how people actually text. Part 1.6 pulled the Latin closer. This part makes the model use what it already has: it rewrites each message in Thaana before answering. On 200 held-out Latin-typed chat messages, it gets the gist of 87%, close to Gemini Flash's 91.5%, and fully understands 48%, against Flash's 69%. Flash is the best Dhivehi model we've measured.

Most Dhivehi on phones is typed in Latin letters: kn irakah tha, vvv reethi, mashah neynge. Gemma 4 can't read it. It can read Thaana. So we taught it to rewrite your message in Thaana before answering.

Lots of chat data taught it the form

GPT-6.1 sol wrote 11,500 short messages a Maldivian might send an assistant, each with its Thaana original, an English meaning and a good reply. A second model retyped every message two or three more ways in Latin.

Gemma learned what a Dhivehi reply looks like. Replies in Thaana went from 20% to 95%, and Sinhala replies from 38 in 120 to one. Full understanding barely moved. Ask about last night's football and it tells you late games ruin your sleep.

Flash's thinking taught it to guess

Gemini Flash shows a summary of its thinking. It names the language, glosses each word, notes what it can't know, then answers. We distilled 4,500 of those thoughts into Gemma. It learned to gloss, and glossed wrong, because a gloss only works if you know the words.

Thaana first worked

Part 1 found that Gemma understands Thaana. A Thaana sentence lands in the same place inside the model as its English translation. Latin-typed Dhivehi doesn't get there. The knowledge is in the weights; Latin letters just don't reach it in one pass. So we gave it two passes:

Why it works

If this is right, the transliteration should decide everything. It does. When the model writes the right Thaana, the reply gets the gist every time. When it writes the wrong Thaana, 61% of the time. Its Thaana is right about 40% of the time, so that's the next thing to train.

Where it still trips

Someone typed bin heylun for bin helun. Helun is shaking, so bin helun is an earthquake. Heylun is waking up. Our model wrote exactly what was typed and explained how to clear land for building. Flash read it as an earthquake. The next round teaches the model to doubt a reading that makes no sense:

Thaana:       ބިން ހޭލުން އެހެނީ ކިހިނެއް
Literal:      how does the earth wake up?
Check:        odd
Likely meant: heylun → ހެލުން (shaking), earthquake
Meaning:      how do earthquakes happen?

Chat shorthand goes in the same round. vvv reethi is varah varah varah reethi, very very very pretty; the model read vvv as "that's why".

Both models make things up. Asked today's date, ours said 22 May 2025 and Flash said 30 March 2026. It was 7 October.

4-bit hides this

At full precision, letting the model write longer replies lifted its score by 25 points. The 4-bit copy gained nothing: its long replies looped and dropped Latin letters into Thaana words. If you score a small language on a quantized model, check this first.

It cuts the other way too. Adapters trained on the 4-bit weights got worse when served at full precision, and started dropping Korean and Bengali letters into replies. The adapter trained at full precision from the start held up best.

Limits

  • The step-by-step numbers are on the 40 messages we developed against. The comparison with Flash uses 200 held-out messages.
  • The judge, gpt-6-luna, agreed with my own blind ratings on about two thirds of replies.
  • This measures reading casual Latin-typed chat. Flash knows far more and writes better Dhivehi.
  • Setup: Gemma 4 26B-A4B with a LoRA adapter; about 2,700 Thaana-first thinking rows, 4,300 synthetic chat rows, 1,500 fiction exchanges and 400 English replay rows; one epoch; every row checked for overlap with the test.
