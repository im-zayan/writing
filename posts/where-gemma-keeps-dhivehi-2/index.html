Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2: write it in Thaana first

October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

Summary. Part 1 found that Gemma 4 understands Dhivehi written in Thaana but not Dhivehi typed in Latin letters, which is how people actually text. Part 1.6 pulled the Latin closer. This part makes the model use what it already has: it rewrites each message in Thaana before answering. On 200 held-out Latin-typed chat messages, it now gets the gist of 87%, close to Gemini Flash's 91.5%, and fully understands 48%, against Flash's 69%. Flash is the best Dhivehi model I've found.

Nobody texts in Thaana. People type kn irakah tha, vvv reethi, mashah neynge. Gemma 4 can't read that, but it can read Thaana. So I made it rewrite each message in Thaana before answering.

Chat data taught it to sound Dhivehi

GPT-6.1 sol wrote 11,500 messages a Maldivian might send a chatbot, each with its Thaana, its English meaning and a reply. Another model retyped each message a few more ways, since people spell the same word differently.

Gemma learned to sound Dhivehi. Thaana replies went from 20% to 95%, and Sinhala replies from 38 in 120 to one. It still didn't understand the messages. Ask about last night's football and it says late games ruin your sleep.

Flash's thinking taught it to guess

Flash shows a summary of its thinking. It names the language, glosses each word, notes what it can't know, then answers. I trained Gemma on 4,500 of these. Gemma learned to gloss, but glossed wrong. Glossing only helps if you know the words.

Writing it in Thaana first worked

Part 1 showed that Gemma understands Thaana: a Thaana sentence lands in the same place inside the model as its English translation. Latin-typed Dhivehi doesn't. The knowledge is there, but Gemma can't reach it from Latin letters in one step. So I gave it two:

Why it works

If this is right, the Thaana it writes should decide the outcome. It does. With the right Thaana, it gets the gist every time. With the wrong Thaana, 61% of the time. Its Thaana is right about 40% of the time, so that's what to fix next.

Where it still fails

Typos break it. Someone typed bin heylun for bin helun. Helun means shaking, so bin helun is an earthquake. Heylun means waking up. My model wrote down exactly what was typed, then explained how to clear land for building. Flash read it as an earthquake. The next round teaches the model to check a reading that makes no sense:

Thaana:       ބިން ހޭލުން އެހެނީ ކިހިނެއް
Literal:      how does the earth wake up?
Check:        odd
Likely meant: heylun → ހެލުން (shaking), earthquake
Meaning:      how do earthquakes happen?

Shorthand is the same problem. vvv reethi is varah varah varah reethi, very very very pretty. My model read vvv as "that's why".

Both models also make things up. Asked the date, mine said 22 May 2025 and Flash said 30 March 2026. It was 7 October.

4-bit hides the gains

I run the model in 4-bit. At full precision, longer replies raised its score by 25 points. In 4-bit they added nothing: long replies looped and put Latin letters inside Thaana words. If you test a small language on a quantized model, check this first.

It cuts the other way too. Adapters trained on the 4-bit weights got worse when served at full precision, and started dropping Korean and Bengali letters into replies. The adapter trained at full precision from the start held up best.

Caveats

  • The step-by-step numbers are on the 40 messages I developed against. The comparison with Flash uses 200 held-out messages.
  • The grader is gpt-6-luna. It agreed with my blind ratings about two thirds of the time.
  • This only tests reading casual Latin-typed chat. Flash knows far more and writes better Dhivehi.
  • Setup: Gemma 4 26B-A4B with a LoRA adapter; about 2,700 Thaana-first thinking rows, 4,300 synthetic chat rows, 1,500 fiction exchanges and 400 English replay rows; one epoch; every row checked for overlap with the test.
