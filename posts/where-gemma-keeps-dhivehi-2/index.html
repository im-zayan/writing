Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2: the bets

October 5, 2026 · 5 min read

In Part 1 I poked around inside Gemma 4 26B-A4B and found that it already understands Thaana, sort of, and gets about halfway with Dhivehi typed in Latin letters, the way people actually text.

How I broke the last one

The first model I grafted Dhivehi into answered "explain a black hole" with a COVID news bulletin. What went wrong was simple, in hindsight. I bolted 32,000 Thaana tokens onto the vocabulary and trained those new tokens and the rest of the model at the same time, on news. The new tokens meant nothing yet, so the rest of the model learned to work around them. And it learned that working around them in one register: news.

No amount of chat fine-tuning afterwards undid it, because the problem was in how the model represented Dhivehi, not in how it behaved. So this time I changed the order.

The order

Teach the new words first, and touch nothing else. For every one of the 32,000 new tokens, I find real sentences that use it, and nudge only that token's embedding until the model "feels" the same reading the single token as it does reading the word spelled out the old way. The rest of the model is frozen.

Then learn from Nau. Nau is a small open model that's good at Dhivehi. My graft uses its exact tokenizer, so at every position I can make my model match Nau's entire guess about the next token, not just the right answer. That gives far more signal per sentence. Still only the new tokens train.

The text isn't mostly news this time. A quarter is real Latin-script comments from Maldivians. Almost a third is chat and instructions. The rest is fiction, religious and legal text, some English, and only 10% news.

Only then touch the body, a little, and anchor it. English is tied to what the original model would have said, so it can't drift. I also add a loss that pulls Dhivehi into the shared "meaning" layers I found in Part 1. I'm trying two versions:

  • Pull Latin Dhivehi toward Thaana Dhivehi.
  • Pull both toward the spot where the model already keeps the meaning of the same sentence in English, Hindi, Russian, Arabic and Japanese. That's the concept centroid from Part 1.

Homographs go in with their other sense in the same batch. mee kon maheh? (which month is this?) sits right next to mee kon kahala maheh? (what kind of fish is this?), so the only way to tell them apart is to actually read the context.

What counts as success

I'll count this run as a success if Dhivehi, in both scripts, activates the right concepts inside the model, even if the Dhivehi it writes is still stiff or ungrammatical. Multilingual models keep meaning in a shared, largely language-agnostic space in their middle layers, where the same concept in different languages is represented by the same features (Wendler et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2025; Dumas et al. 2025; Anthropic 2025). If Dhivehi lands on those features, the model can use what it already knows. Grammar and stiffness can be fixed later with fine-tuning.

The bets

All of these are for the model at the end of this run, before any chat training. Where I can, I'm comparing against Nau, the plain 26B, and the numbers from Part 1.

It'll learn Dhivehi, but won't catch Nau.

  • Dhivehi bits per character goes from 2.82 (right after the graft) to 0.5–0.7. Nau sits at 0.32 after 700 million tokens of training; I'm using about 25 million.
  • English bits per character comes back to within 0.05 of the original 0.71.
  • The model's surprise per Dhivehi word drops from 18 to under 10. Nau is at 7.

Latin Dhivehi moves into the shared space.

  • My twelve hand-typed Latin sentences go from finding their meaning 50% of the time to at least 80%.
  • Thaana gets tighter. Its similarity to the shared centroid goes from 0.74 to at least 0.85.
  • On 200 held-out human transliterations, Latin→Thaana matching goes from 5.5% to 20–40%.
  • The concept-centroid version beats Latin→Thaana alignment. Pulling Latin toward Thaana copies Thaana's own wonkiness; pulling both toward the real centroid shouldn't.

The fish.

  • In Thaana, ކޮން ކަހަލަ މަހެއް reads as fish rather than month.
  • In Latin, kon kahala maheh moves toward fish but doesn't fully get there. Latin has less to lean on.
  • ހަކުރު (sugar), which the plain model didn't recognise at all, gets into the top 100 for "sugar" but not the top 10. Nau can teach how the word is used. Teaching what it means is the job of the next run.

Tasks.

  • Turning Latin into Thaana: error rate from 0.57 to under 0.30. Nau is at 0.11, so still a long way off.
  • Dhivehi translation: chrF++ from 27.5 to 35 or more, close to Nau's 40.8.
  • Understanding Dhivehi, multiple-choice, translation into English: no worse than now. The plain model is already decent at these.
  • Agent tasks stay bad, about 4 out of 24. Nothing here teaches tool use; that comes with chat training.

How I'll know I was wrong

If the alignment version doesn't beat plain distillation on the shared-space numbers, then Nau is doing all the work and the shared layers aren't helping.

What's next either way

Two things are queued after this. First, a dictionary pointer: every dictionary word gets nudged until the model treats it like its English meaning in context, and words like މަސް point at both fish and month. Second, a map of where Dhivehi and English disagree word by word inside the model, so the next round of training can go after the worst words instead of everything at once.

Grammar, register and being a good chat partner come last, with fine-tuning.

Results in Part 3. I'll grade every bet above.
