Where does Gemma keep Dhivehi? Part 2.9: subtracting the average

October 10, 2026 · 9 min read

The CPT run improved translation, but Latin Dhivehi was still weak. I kept both checkpoints fixed and went back to cross-script matching, this time with an unpaired map from Latin to Thaana.

The map failed. A simpler step worked much better: subtracting each script’s average raised stock Gemma’s matching score from 17% to 46%.

A new matching test

I used a new bank of 512 Latin/Thaana pairs. At a fixed layer, I averaged the states across each sentence, then picked the closest Thaana state for each Latin input. Only the correct sentence in first place counted. A random choice would get about 0.2% right.

This score measures matching among those 512 choices. I tested translation separately below.

Subtract each script’s average

I first collected states from 1,024 other sentences in each script. The Latin and Thaana groups used different examples, so this step had no matching pairs to learn from.

I found each group’s average state and subtracted it from every state in that script. I then compared their directions, giving each the same length. The raw-state comparison used equal lengths too; only the subtraction was new.

That is centering: describe each sentence by how it differs from its group’s average.

The two groups may sit in different parts of the space. Centering puts their averages at the same point. I can then compare how each sentence differs from its own average, without knowing the matching pairs.

The average may reflect script, style, length or topic. This test does not tell me which caused the gain.

Centering helped

Here are stock PT and the same step-1,800 CPT checkpoint, both at layer 9.

Centering raised the original model’s score from 17.19% to 46.29%, and CPT from 17.97% to 50.78%. Resampling the test sentences put both gains well above zero. The results section below gives the intervals.

I also used 64 separate known pairs to learn a map, while keeping it close to the original coordinates. It reached 47.46% and 51.37%. Those small gains over centering were uncertain.

A learned map now has a stronger baseline to beat.

The unpaired map failed here

Unpaired Rosetta prompted this experiment. Its authors show that separately trained models can sometimes arrange related content in similar ways. Their method uses that shape to match two sets of states without known pairs.

I tried their code on Latin Dhivehi and Thaana. Both sets came from the same model, unlike the paper’s separately trained models. Rosetta also centers the states, then learns a shared rotation or reflection between the sets.

Its three runs found 0, 2 and 2 correct matches out of 512 for the original model, and 0, 3 and 2 for CPT. Centering alone had scored about 46% and 51%.

The second layer, also chosen before the test, gave the same result.

This run used fewer examples and less computation than the paper’s main setup. With only 1,024 sentences per group in 2,816 dimensions, many directions have little data to guide them. I do not know which part caused the failure. This map did not help.

Messages people type

The main dataset does not say how each Latin sentence was made. Formal text may miss the spelling changes and shortcuts in ordinary messages.

I checked 64 public comments typed in Latin letters, using the same maps without refitting. Nau had produced their Thaana versions. A person had not checked these targets for this test.

Centering helped the original pretrained model, CPT and the stock chat model. The unpaired maps stayed near chance. Maps guided by the 64 formal-text pairs did not consistently beat centering here either.

The translation patch did not help

I then asked the stock chat model to translate.

I fitted a separate map inside the translation prompt. Then I changed the state at the last Latin source token after layer 9 and let the model continue. I tried centering, the learned map and several controls. The other states still came from Latin input.

Centering is a useful baseline for comparing scripts. It has not given me a better Latin translator.

At layer 9, mean-pooled CKA was 0.475 for stock PT, 0.501 for CPT and 0.463 for the chat model. CKA compares the arrangement of matched examples within the two sets. After a linear adjustment for log token count, the values were 0.464, 0.487 and 0.431. Shared structure survived that adjustment, but did not make this unpaired fit succeed. The finite-sample null was above zero: stock PT had a shuffled-label mean of 0.211. There were 100 permutations per layer.

A separate English/Sinhala control used 197 test candidates. Its centered layer-9 matching scores were 98.48% for stock PT and CPT, and 96.95% for the chat model. These were different examples, with a different candidate count; they are not a matched-difficulty comparison with Dhivehi. That control used 100 known pairs for its supervised maps and did not run the unpaired solver.

For generation, mean sentence chrF was 20.33 for clean Latin, 73.97 for clean Thaana, 20.50 for the centering control, 17.61 for the unpaired map and 34.69 for the matching-Thaana oracle. chrF measures character-fragment overlap with the reference. The unpaired map’s change from clean Latin was −2.71, with a paired 95% interval of −7.20 to +1.31. The oracle’s change was +14.36, with +5.02 to +25.17. The raw example and meaning ratings show why that latter gain is not full recovery.

Data, models and method

Data. The formal bank comes from alakxender/dhivehi-transliteration-pairs. I used 1,024 fitting rows per script from different pairs, 64 separate paired anchors, 128 validation pairs and 512 test pairs. The dataset does not say how every Latin row was made. I screened the test set for exact matches and contained phrases in available training texts and earlier probes. That can miss paraphrases and repeated stories. I do not know what the original model saw during pretraining.

Models. I used Gemma 4 26B-A4B checkpoints with default 4-bit quantization and 8-bit overrides for some modules. CPT adds the step-1,800 adapter at scale 0.5 to the pretrained checkpoint. Generation uses the separate stock instruction-tuned checkpoint. I computed means and maps for each model and trained no model weights in this experiment.

Centering and maps. Means come only from the two unpaired fitting pools. Both raw and centered rows are normalized to unit length before cosine comparison; the centered version subtracts the fitting mean first. The official Unpaired Rosetta code is pinned at that commit. I used 30 clusters, 10 restarts, batch size 512, 30 refinement iterations and seeds 42, 1337 and 20261010. There is no dimension reduction. This is a reduced-compute application of the method, not a replication of the paper’s main settings. The paired baselines are separate Procrustes fits; the identity-regularized version adds an identity matrix to the normalized anchor cross-covariance before taking its polar factor. The shuffled version permutes the anchor correspondences.

Generation patch. Its map uses the last source-token state in the exact English-translation chat template, with thinking disabled. It uses the same fitting rows and separate anchors, not the 17 probes. The mapped direction is restored with the target fitting mean and median fitting-state radius. The centering control uses the identity rotation with that same restoration; it is not just subtracting a vector and passing the result onward. Only the last source-token residual after layer 9 changes during prefill. Earlier cached states and other positions stay as computed from Latin. Greedy decoding stops at a genuine end token or 48 new tokens. Replacing a state with itself reproduced the clean output exactly on all 17 cases.

Probes and ratings. Five cases reuse short sense probes; twelve use Nau-generated Thaana and Latin made with a dictionary and rules. They are not a new human-checked test. Two language-model reviewers saw 86 distinct outputs under shuffled labels. I showed duplicate strings once. They agreed on 83 ratings and flagged fish/month ambiguity, a location mismatch and an English gender choice. The weather case illustrates a partial change; it does not stand in for the full set.

Numerics. A preliminary check found that padding and batch shape could change the saved states, so all study inference ran one unpadded input at a time with floating parameters in float32. The quantized integer weights were unchanged. Two paired-control fits failed an orthogonality check because their float32 singular-value decompositions returned invalid factors. I recomputed those same factors in float64 and kept the failed originals and repair records. The data, shuffle and regularizer stayed fixed; none of the unpaired maps changed. Independent checks reproduced the retrieval calculation from saved states and verified the generation outputs, identities and ratings.
